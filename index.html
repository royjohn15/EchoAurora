    What it measures

    RT60 (reverberation time) is how long a sound takes to fade by 60 dB. Long reverberation makes each syllable smear into the next. Classrooms should be at or below 0.6 s (ANSI/ASA S12.60, rooms under 283 m³) or 0.7 s (283–566 m³).

    C50 (clarity) compares the sound energy arriving in the first 50 ms, which helps you understand words, with everything that arrives later, which blurs them. Above 0 dB speech is usually clear; below about −3 dB it becomes hard to follow.

    Both are ratios within one recording, so the phone microphone doesn't need level calibration.

    How a clap becomes numbers

    The phone keeps the last quarter-second of sound and starts recording when a sound rises 30 dB above the room's background. It keeps 3 s of decay, splits it into five octave bands (250 Hz to 4 kHz) with time-reversed filters, and subtracts the background noise. It then integrates the energy backwards (Schroeder's method), with a tail correction after the decay meets the noise (after Lundeby).

    T20: a straight-line fit from −5 to −25 dB, multiplied by 3. The headline RT60 is the average of the 500 Hz, 1 kHz and 2 kHz bands, the ones that matter most for speech.

    Checks on every clap

    Microphone: the decay must reach at least 35 dB above the background (30 dB is accepted with a caution), and the fitted line must be straight (r ≥ 0.99). Echo cancellation, noise suppression and auto-gain are switched off.

    Accelerometer: a jolt when the sound starts means something hit the desk or the phone, not a clap in the air, so that clap is rejected.

    Gyroscope + accelerometer: if the phone was moving during the 3 s, the clap is rejected.

    Second sound: a new sound arriving during the decay (a cough, a door) is rejected.

    Repeatability: the room table shows how far apart repeat claps at the same seat are.

    Limits

    A clap has little energy below 500 Hz, so the 250 Hz band is often too quiet. A balloon pop works better there. Phone microphones and their internal processing vary, so compare with a second clap and, if you can, a second phone. The fix-it advice uses Sabine's formula, which assumes sound is spread evenly through the room. Treat the areas as a starting estimate.
